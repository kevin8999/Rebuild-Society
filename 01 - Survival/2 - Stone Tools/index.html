    Stone Tools

    Stone tools are the ancient toolkit that propelled humanity forward, shaping our ability to survive and thrive. From basic rocks to finely crafted blades, these tools were the first step in our technological journey.

    Invented: 3.3 million BCE1

    Uses - Hunting and butchering - Cutting, carving - Chopping trees - Defend against hostile humans and animals

    Requirements - [ ] Tool-making rock. Basically, a rock that is: - [ ] Homogeneous - the rock has the same structure throughout the entire rock - [ ] Brittle - the rock needs to break apart with a crack. It should not crumble into tiny pieces - [ ] Elastic - the flake bends off and snaps back which makes it easier to get - [ ] Isotropic - no matter where you hit the rock, it cracks open the same - [ ] Hammer stone - used to hit the rock to knock off chunks of it. It must weigh less than the rock you're trying to hit

    Optional requirements - [ ] Rope - to make your stone weapons more sturdy

    Good tool-making rocks include flint, quartz, obsidian, chert, jasper, basalt, sandstone, and granite.

    [!note] Note Until you have a stable food supply, you will be relying on stone tools to hunt for animals and to gather food.

    See 02.01 - Agriculture to learn how to get a steady food supply without needing to go out and hunt.

    Methods

    To make a basic cutter: 1. Hold the tool-making rock securely, away from your body, and strike it with the hammer stone. This action should cause a flake to detach from the tool-making rock. This flake will serve as the cutting edge of your basic cutter. 2. Test the flake by cutting something

    If you wish to shape the flake into a specific tool, such as a knife or scraper, you can further refine its shape through a process called knapping. To knap, hold the tool-making rock securely and use the hammer stone to carefully chip away at the edges of the flake, shaping it into the desired form. Take your time and be precise to achieve the desired shape and sharpness.

    To improve the effectiveness of your cutter, cut out a hole inside a long piece of wood (approximately one forearm length long). Then, lodge the rock inside the hole. Make sure the sharp side is sticking out from the wood. Now, you can swing that tool and get more power from your stone tools.

    We've also attached the following guides to teach you how to knap: 1. Flint Knapping - Basic Concepts 2. Flint Knapping - Pressure Flaking 3. Flint Knapping - Notching and Fluting
